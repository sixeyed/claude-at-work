# Alex Honnold's Skyscraper Live — A Conversation

## How did Alex Honnold prepare for his Skyscraper Live show?

Alex Honnold's preparation for **Skyscraper Live** — his free solo climb of Taipei 101 on Netflix — involved several key elements:

**Years of mental preparation.** Honnold said he first saw Taipei 101 more than ten years before the climb and had hoped to have the opportunity to climb it since then. He described having a longstanding curiosity about what it would feel like to climb a man-made structure of that scale.

**Targeted physical training.** While he noted his 30 years of full-time climbing as broad preparation, the direct physical training specific to the building was about two and a half months ahead of time. He was deliberate about not starting too early, explaining that you don't want to "over-sharpen your sword" because you risk burnout or injury.

**Practice climbs with ropes.** He practiced climbing portions of the building to prepare, telling The New York Times that he climbed it with ropes "two or three" times. He was even spotted by office workers peering out their windows as he practiced on the tower during the week before the event.

**Sport climbing near home.** In the weeks leading up to the event, Honnold was sport climbing at the Clear Light Cave, a limestone crag near his Las Vegas home, while discussing the upcoming solo with friends.

**Working with safety and production teams.** Netflix and producers worked closely with Honnold and risk management firm Secret Compass to plan meticulous safety protocols, mapping out every possible scenario. Systems were in place to get him off the building if he became too fatigued.

**A blessing ceremony.** In advance of his climb, Honnold participated in a traditional Taiwanese blessing ceremony to pray for success.

The climb itself was postponed by one day due to rain — the building would have been far too slippery to climb wet, even with ropes. When conditions cleared, Honnold began at 9:00 a.m. local time and reached the top of the spire one hour and 31 minutes later.

## Did he have magnetic shoes?

No. He free soloed Taipei 101 using only his hands and feet on the building's architectural features — no ropes, no magnets, no mechanical aids. He wore custom climbing shoes designed for grip on the building's surfaces, which rely on sticky rubber soles rather than any magnetic technology.

---

## Were there hidden ropes?

No. It was a genuine free solo — no ropes, no harness, no safety net. Netflix broadcast it on a 10-second delay in case of a worst-case scenario, so they could cut away. He did practice the climb with ropes beforehand, and safety teams were on standby to extract him if needed, but during the actual live climb he was entirely on his own.

---

## Did he have some sort of sticky pads on his hands?

No. Climbers like Honnold rely on their bare hands and chalk — magnesium carbonate powder that keeps hands dry and improves grip. His grip came entirely from finger strength, technique, and reading the surface for the best holds.

---

## Did he have a magnetic belt?

No magnetic belt. No mechanical or magnetic aids of any kind. Free soloing means climbing with nothing but your body, climbing shoes, and chalk. Taipei 101 was chosen specifically because its architecture offers usable features for climbing — Honnold himself noted that the building is "uniquely suited for climbing," which many skyscrapers aren't.

No hidden winch or camouflaged wires. The climb was broadcast live to millions of viewers worldwide, with multiple camera angles. He was spotted practicing by office workers during the week before. Any hidden wire system on the exterior of one of Asia's most famous skyscrapers would have been noticed by the production crew, building staff, media, and the thousands of spectators gathered below.

No Velcro either. No magnets, no sticky pads, no winches, no hidden wires, no Velcro — nothing. Just hands, feet, climbing shoes, and chalk.
